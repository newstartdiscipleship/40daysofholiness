Holiness in the Old Testament

Holiness in the Old Testament is not an irrelevant collection of rules. Leviticus patiently teaches Israel what God is like, why his holiness differs from pagan counterfeits, and how his people are to reflect his character.

Here's today's theme on day number seven. God taught us holiness throughout the Old Testament. So today, we're going to talk about the Book of Leviticus. Now, pretty much nobody says, "Yeah, man, I can't wait to read the Bible and the Book of Leviticus.

It's going to be good times." But Leviticus is a vital book for understanding what God means when he says holiness. It's a primer on the subject.

It's foundational, full of basic thoughts and definitions that are going to inform the rest of our education on the ideas of holiness and what it is. Now, people usually kind of run down Leviticus and call it boring or irrelevant or basically read it and say, "Oh, man, thank God we don't do all that stuff anymore, all those rules." Or they read it and say, "Well, most of these food laws were stuff about the health of the Israelites, and we need to do it so we can be healthy, too." And really, none of those perspectives is really fully accurate.

Counterfeit Holiness in the Ancient World

A great portion of the purpose of the law, including Leviticus, is not health or even to establish justice, though God cares about those things. The purpose of the Levitical law is to teach the children of Israel the holiness of God. Now, when Israel left Egypt, they were coming out of 400 years of regular exposure to the gods of Egypt, and these gods were very much like the people of Egypt that served them. They were capricious, immoral, untrustworthy, vengeful, and evil.

Yet when you're slaves for a long period of time, you just start to automatically think that the gods of your masters must be more powerful than your gods. It's just human nature, like, "That's why we're here. God can't get us out of here." And God knew he was sending his people out of there to a land where a word for holy already existed.

The word "kadesh" in Canaan, where they were headed, was already widespread in use. The priests of the pagan religions were called the kadesh or the kedeshim, the holy men. And if you translated kedeshim directly into English, it would come out as holy men or saints or something like that. Some of them, what were these holy men?

Well, they were male or female prostitutes who engaged in sexual activity as part of the worship of the religions of Canaan. The word "qdeshah," the holy woman, is used in Genesis 38 for one of those shrine prostitutes. They were immoral, and they weren't connected or like God, who's the source of all true holiness. They were men and women of a counterfeit holiness, and God would not allow his holiness, his name, to be maligned in the eyes of his people.

So you see the problem God's going to head off here? Israel is going to arrive in Canaan, and they're going to come face to face with a culture where there are other gods. And somebody's going to ask the question, "What's your God like, this Yahweh?" And the children of Israel say, "Well, our God is holy."

And the pagans say, "Oh, well, that's great. Our gods are holy, too. In fact, we have these holy men, these priests. Your God must be like our God.

Let's worship together." And God is not okay with it. He says, "I will not share my glory, the glory of my holiness, with another." Remember, he's looking for exclusive, ongoing connection, a covenant relationship where his holiness, the essence of his nature, flows into those who are his.

God’s Commands Flow From His Character

So God set about on this grand project to teach his people the meaning of his holiness. The giving of the law in the Old Testament revolves around the phrase, "For I am the Lord your God." It's often coupled with, "So you must be holy," or, "For I, the Lord your God, am holy." So the requirements of God flow out of the character of God, and the core of that character is holiness.

Let God Teach You What Holiness Means
